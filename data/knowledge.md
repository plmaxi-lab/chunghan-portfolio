## 4. Case study: AI Guided Onboarding (Splunk, 2025)

**Before:** Customers needed to connect Splunk to their existing infrastructure, and the same data often had to serve security, compliance, and operations at once, each with different setup needs. The real pain was solution discovery: "I don't know which Splunk solutions and setup I need for my use case." It added up to a 45+ day time-to-value problem.

**Where AI fits:** He used jobs-to-be-done to pick where AI creates the most value. Three jobs, three initiatives: onboarding ("which solutions should I use?"), cleansing ("how do I clean my data faster?"), and troubleshooting ("what's broken and how do I fix it?").

**Journey:**
1. The first vision was an AI-generated infrastructure map with configuration guidance. Feedback was positive, but what the map shows depends on each customer's infrastructure, which the team could not know up front.
2. The project stalled for three weeks: design asked engineering for specs the traditional way, and engineering said they needed to know the desired output first. Chung-Han stepped in and reset the team: design for the AI, not around it. Start from the output you want, then work back to the prompt.
3. He set a framework of three angles: how people start (content initialization), how people interact (interaction model), and how information surfaces (information architecture).
4. The first information architecture worked for the system but not for users, so the team switched to a task-based model that separates learning from doing.
5. The team built variations on a 2x2: conversational vs. canvas, intent vs. template.

**Research:** 15 high-profile customers plus the solutions engineering team. Findings: chatbot-only interaction feels limited and users lose trust while waiting; not everyone knows what they want, so show a smart default instead of asking many questions; a structured flow is still essential for accurate recommendations.

**Decision:** Canvas-native, intent-driven, with prefilled defaults, and AI as a supporting layer rather than a chatbot gatekeeper.

**Outcome:** Demoed at .conf 2025 with positive feedback and validated with 15 customers. Alpha was planned for Q2 2026 and GA for 2026. The customer sessions were a preference test, not task-completion research, because customers could not bring their real environments. Chung-Han left in a reorg before launch, so there is no post-launch data.

**Autonomy roadmap:** first AI suggests; then it supports custom use cases and validates with sample data; then in-app setup and status, with AI configuring and monitoring. Each step hands the AI more control only once trust is earned.

**What he would do differently:** agree on the success metric before design; build a sandbox with customer-like data so testing measures real task completion; treat it like an eval, with a fixed set of scenarios run on every iteration.

## 5. Case study: Auto-Schematization (Splunk, 2025)

**Goal:** free users from data prep so they can focus on insights.

**Before:** field extraction relied on regex: machine-readable, not human-friendly.

**Building trust in AI:** AI analysis is not instant, so it starts early in the background, shows progress, and has a defined timeout. Human in the loop: AI suggests, the human decides, and users can compare options and see the match rate for each.

**Surprise:** users said "It works, but I could just use Regex101. What's the difference?" Field extraction alone was not enough.

**Pivot:** stop extracting fields, start enabling outcomes. The AI now scans sample data, recommends matching Splunk data models, shows which fields it found, and flags fields missing for compliance. Since one dataset can serve many needs, users see groups of patterns and shape a data model per group. Auto Field Extraction shipped in Q1 2026.

## 6. Case study: Root Cause Analysis and Self-Healing Pipelines (Splunk, 2025)

**Before:** "I know something's broken. I just don't know what, or where to start."

**Goal:** make AI reasoning transparent, trusted, and actionable.

**Trust principles:** the user triggers the analysis when ready, so they stay in control; the result explains itself as a summary, then details, then a recommended fix, in plain language; a side-by-side comparison shows exactly what will change and why.

**Outcome:** positive feedback at .conf 2025, with GA planned for 2026. The longer-term vision is standardized, automated playbook responses.

**Why it matters:** this is his clearest human-in-the-loop example. The design work is deciding where AI acts on its own, where it stops for a human, and how a user can see, check, and undo what it did. He calls the approach "assertive AI": AI surfaces, explains, and recommends, and the human decides. It is the scaffolding that makes agentic AI safe to adopt.

## 7. Case study: Unified Data Acquisition (Splunk, 2022 to 2023)

- **Before:** 100+ ingestion flows built in silos. Every new data source was a custom integration, setup took from a day to weeks, and support tickets spiked with each new source.
- **Insight:** the problem was not individual screens. Every source was being designed from scratch, with 1,000+ possible setup paths across cloud providers, services, sources, and methods.
- **Solution:** one shared framework and portal, so any source uses the same patterns. He borrowed the step-by-step device onboarding model from his smart home work. Midway, the team found that consistent workflow was not enough: two designers built the same Azure input and got different forms, so he made consistent content the standard too.
- **Team:** he led three designers, one per cloud provider plus a standards owner, with a weekly sync across partner teams that did not report to him.
- **Outcome:** 400+ customers with an active data input, average setup of 13 minutes (down from days or weeks), 61% of pipelines active daily, 1,000+ sources on one framework, and a new connector (CrowdStrike) integrated in 1.5 months.
- **Lesson:** consistency at scale does not come from syncing more. It comes from designing the framework first.

## 8. Case study: Unified Data Observability, a strategy pivot (Splunk, 2024)

- **Why this problem:** Splunk's own survey ranked "easy troubleshooting" the #1 unmet need four years in a row.
- **Vision:** a live topology map tracing data from source to destination.
- **Blockers:** thousands of nodes make a graph unreadable at enterprise scale, and the status data the team needed did not exist yet. Progress stalled.
- **Turning point:** a lead admin at a customer looked at the prototype and said, "Will I stare at this every day?" Then he answered himself: "No."
- **Insight:** Chung-Han took the team back to jobs-to-be-done research. Most people show up because something broke, or they want a warning before it does. He reframed the problem into a maturity framework: active, reactive, proactive, and automated monitoring.
- **Shipped:** a simpler monitoring dashboard first, in 3 months instead of the 6+ months the original path would have taken, with alerts and AI-assisted troubleshooting sequenced after it.
- **Outcome:** 120+ weekly active users, and the framework became how the roadmap was prioritized.
- **Buy-in:** he led with research, not opinion. He never killed the topology vision; he sequenced it.

## 9. Leadership and ways of working

- **Style:** player-coach. Hands-on in the hardest problems, coaching everywhere else. He prototypes the direction on the hardest problem to set the bar, then the team owns execution.
- **Placing people:** he teaches to each person's nature rather than one fixed standard. Some people are strongest at vision, others at execution, and he places them where their strength meets the bar.
- **Quality bar:** consistency first, held through regular critiques, the design system, and reviews with solutions engineers, so quality means real customer value, not just polish.
- **Growing people:** he maps work against the leveling guideline point by point, points people to targeted learning, and leans into each person's strengths. He also advocates for his people's visibility with leadership.
- **Distributed teams:** async-first rituals, with overlap hours protected for what truly needs live time.
- **New domains:** sit with users, shadow on-call, use the product on real problems.
- **Measuring success:** agree on the metric before design starts: time to value, adoption, support load.
- **Cross-functional:** bring evidence, often from customers or solutions engineers, and land decisions together with product and engineering. He has driven adoption of new practices (Pendo analytics, jobs-to-be-done) across product and engineering teams that did not report to him.
- **Through a merger:** he led his team through the integration period after Cisco acquired Splunk.

## 10. AI: how he thinks about it and how he uses it

- **AI-native, his definition:** the workflow is redesigned around what AI can do, and the design work is deciding where the human stays in control. It is not adding a chatbot to an old product.
- **Core question:** trust. On production systems, efficiency is not the hard part. Show the user what the AI did and why, let them decide, and widen autonomy step by step.
- **His own practice:** he builds with Claude Code every week. For Teappo he built an event permit form filler (TFF Filler), a social post generator, and a compliance calendar. He built his portfolio presentation in code, and this Cha Cha assistant too.
- At Splunk he used Claude Code to make workflow animations for leadership reviews, hosted a monthly AI learning club, and brought in a code-based design tool to show the team.
- Figma and Figma Make for early co-creation with product managers and engineers; different models for research synthesis and reading requirements.
- He has experimented with a design system in code but has not shipped one. Do not overclaim this.

**TFF Filler story:** Teappo does many events, and every event needs a temporary food facility permit form. His first attempt was too ambitious (a full web app with login and AI) and ran out of steam. He cut it to a small version for two forms: upload, detect fields, fill. Then he debugged OCR and signature issues with targeted tests. Lessons: start small, and domain knowledge still matters even when AI does the heavy lifting.
